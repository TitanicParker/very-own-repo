Episode Eleven

A Correction Is Not a Rewind

Two conversations can end with the same sentence and contain different histories of intelligibility.

Watch what happens to Tuesday.

First history:

“Meeting Tuesday.”

A minute later:

“Sorry — I misread the invitation. Thursday.”

Something in the first landing was faulty.

The live field already contained the invitation.

The speaker recovered it incorrectly.

Thursday repairs what Tuesday got wrong.

Second history:

“Meeting Tuesday.”

Two hours later:

“They’ve moved it. Thursday.”

Here Tuesday may have been completely right when it landed.

The field changed later.

Thursday does not repair the earlier reading.

It completes a new demand under new conditions.

Surface form can hide this difference.

Tuesday becomes Thursday in both cases.

If you only compare the final strings, the histories collapse into one pattern.

But completed intelligibility is not merely the before-and-after text.

It includes what unresolvedness was live and what changed enough to settle it.

In the first history, the unresolvedness was partly inside the recovery itself.

The correction changes the achieved determination because the earlier one was defective.

That is Repair territory.

In the second history, the earlier determination may remain historically correct.

What changed is the world to which the determination must now answer.

The new completion does not erase the old one.

This is why supersession is not proof of prior error.

A later result can replace an earlier result for action without proving that the earlier result was badly formed.

You can feel the difference in explanation.

“Why did you say Tuesday?”

“Because I misread it.”

Versus: “Because it was Tuesday then.”

The route matters because it tells us what kind of reopening occurred.

Was a defect exposed?

Did a condition change?

Did the governing question change?

Without that history, we can still use the latest result.

But we cannot always understand what happened to the earlier one.

Compression helps continuation and can hide diagnosis.

This gives us a more precise picture of inheritance.

An inherited completion can be preserved, repaired, superseded, or reopened under a new demand.

Those are not four new primitives.

They are different histories through which completed intelligibility can remain or become live again.

And once histories can be carried forward, something surprising becomes possible.

A tiny present expression can carry a large amount of completed past.

Most of that past need not be spoken again.

“Usual place. Seven.”

Three words can carry months of prior coordination.

How much can a small thing carry?
