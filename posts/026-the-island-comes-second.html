Episode Twenty-Six

The Island Comes Second

Take one word from the sentence.

blue

There it is.

It looks independent now.

One word on one screen.

You can point to it, quote it, spell it, count its letters, ask what it means.

But notice what made that independence available.

“Blue” is the word I mean.

A relation has made the word into an object for us.

The quotation marks help draw a boundary.

The sentence distinguishes this word from what the word normally does.

It gives us something we can now treat as one thing.

So relation and independence are not opposites.

Relation can produce a form stable enough to be handled as independent.

The same operation happens at larger scales.

A proof arrives at a theorem. The theorem can later be cited in one line.

A meeting reaches a decision. The decision can later appear as one item in a record.

A long route becomes: the usual way.

Something relationally achieved becomes sufficiently bounded, sufficiently determinate, sufficiently persistent that later thought can take it as a unit.

The landing becomes an island.

Then the island enters another relation.

John said that the blue cup is beside the kettle.

The whole earlier sentence can now participate as one term inside a larger intelligibility.

And that larger intelligibility can itself become a term.

Mary doubts John’s claim that the blue cup is beside the kettle.

Whole becomes part. Part enters whole. Again.

This is one way finite local forms can participate in indefinite depth.

Relation can make something one.

That one can be related.

The new relation can become one again.

relation → completion → unit → relation → completion → unit

We usually begin our descriptions later.

There is a person. There is an object. There is a proposition. There is a variable.

Then we ask how those independently given things relate.

That is enormously useful.

It is the basis of counting, measurement, responsibility, reference, proof, engineering, law, and much ordinary thought.

Nothing here asks us to give it up.

But the order may matter.

What if independence is sometimes what a relation has successfully made available?

A boundary can be achieved.

A difference can become determinate.

A dependency can lose its governing force.

A result can stand strongly enough to be carried without rebuilding the event that produced it.

Independence then becomes not the absence of relation, but one possible achievement within a relational field.

An island can be made by the sea around it.

This does not prove that nothing is metaphysically independent.

It makes a smaller claim.

Intelligibility does not get independence for free.

If two things are to count as two things, some difference must be available.

If one thing is to stand alone, enough must distinguish and stabilize it for that standing-alone to be intelligible.

So perhaps the primitive picture should not be:

independent things → relations between them

Perhaps intelligibility often begins earlier:

relation → distinction → stability → usable independence

And once that is visible, one familiar question starts to sound unexpectedly loaded.

Whose demand?
