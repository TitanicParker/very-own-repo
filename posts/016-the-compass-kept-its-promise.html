Episode Sixteen

An Appeal to Reason

Take a compass.

Open it.

Do not measure the opening. Just keep it.

Put the sharp point down.

Turn the compass once around it.

The pencil draws a circle.

One opening has made a world.

The place where the sharp point stood is the centre.

The line made by the pencil is the circumference.

Now put the sharp point anywhere on that circumference.

We will call that place a station.

Nothing mysterious in the word.

It is simply where the compass is standing now.

But keep the opening.

The point has moved.

The opening has not.

Let the pencil touch the circle.

It meets the circumference at another place.

Move the sharp point there.

That is the next station.

Keep the opening.

Do it again.

And again.

And again.

And again.

And again.

Six moves.

Then the compass arrives exactly where the first station was.

The movement closes.

Nobody measured the circumference.

Nobody divided it into six.

Nobody told the compass where the sixth station ought to be.

You preserved one opening and allowed that opening to meet the world it had made.

The world closed around it.

Now join every station to the centre.

Each line is the original compass opening.

Join each station to its neighbour around the circle.

Those lines are the original compass opening too.

Look at what has appeared.

A hexagon.

And inside it:

six equilateral triangles.

Each of those triangles has three equal sides.

Each has three sixty-degree angles.

Across the hexagon, opposite edges give three directions of parallel line.

The little construction has begun producing more than a picture.

It has begun producing a field.

Extend those directions.

Repeat the distance.

Carry the same relations outward.

The original circle can begin populating the plane.

And now something remarkable becomes possible.

Erase the circle.

Go on.

Rub it out.

The hexagon does not forget how it was made.

The triangles do not cease to be equilateral.

The parallel directions do not collapse.

The relations survive the disappearance of the thing that first disclosed them.

The generator can disappear while the generated world remains available.

That is worth looking at for a while.

From those relations, geometry can continue.

Position can become coordinate.

Angle can become ratio.

Ratio can become trigonometry.

Relations can be compressed into symbols.

Symbols can be operated upon.

Algebra becomes possible.

Not because mathematics changes the subject every few pages.

Because something becomes enough to stand upon.

And then something else becomes possible from there.

And something else from there.

The world of mathematics accumulates.

It does not restart.

Now consider a sentence almost everybody meets at school.

Pythagoras' theorem.

a² + b² = c²

Most of us are taught to read it for meaning.

There is a right triangle.

There is a hypotenuse.

There are two other sides.

There is a relation between their squares.

Fine.

But we have learned to ask another question.

What kind of completed intelligibility is this?

Hold the mathematical field steady.

The theorem establishes one privileged relation whose terms remain what they are through their bearing upon one another.

In our coordinates:

Relating × Focal × Bearing.

20.

Now translate the theorem.

English

In a right triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

Chinese

在直角三角形中，斜边的平方等于两条直角边的平方和。

Different sounds.

Different marks.

Different histories of writing.

But the completed intelligibility has not become Chinese.

It has not ceased being English.

It was never English in the first place.

The languages are carrying it.

The words change.

The ordering permitted by a language may change.

The grammar changes.

The characters change.

The sounds change.

The writing direction may change.

The theorem does not therefore move to another position in the 54.

Its landing remains 20.

That should disturb the ordinary picture of language a little.

Because apparently the same completed intelligibility can survive translation.

Not merely its topic.

Not merely “roughly what somebody meant.”

Its structural landing.

Translate it again.

Arabic.

Hindi.

Swahili.

Irish.

Spanish.

A language may have to reorganize the sentence completely to make it natural.

But if Pythagoras' theorem has actually survived the translation, the completed intelligibility has survived too.

So what crossed the language boundary?

Not the English words.

Not the Chinese characters.

Not the Latin alphabet.

Not the sound.

Something intelligible enough to be rebuilt in another human language.

Now let the same question travel through time.

The theorem is ancient.

The people through whom it reached us did not inhabit our world.

Borders moved.

Empires appeared and disappeared.

Languages changed.

Writing changed.

Religions changed.

Cities vanished.

Technologies appeared that would have been unimaginable.

The night sky above them was familiar.

Almost everything underneath it changed.

And yet here you are.

Looking at the same relation.

That is not merely preservation of information.

A dead string of marks would not be enough.

Someone living now can grasp it.

Can draw it.

Can test it.

Can use it.

Can teach it.

Can continue from it.

Something completed in human understanding became available beyond the life in which it was completed.

Then beyond the language in which it was expressed.

Then beyond the society that carried it.

Then beyond the century.

Human beings found a way to let intelligibility outlive them.

That is what standing on the shoulders of giants actually requires.

The giant cannot merely have existed.

Their achievement has to remain available after they are gone.

Otherwise every generation starts on the ground.

A theorem is one way of doing this.

A word is another.

A map.

A recipe.

A distinction.

A musical notation.

A law.

A measurement.

A warning.

A method.

A story told to a child.

Civilization is crowded with things whose original circles have been erased.

We inherit the hexagon.

We say parallel without reconstructing the history of parallelism.

We use zero.

We write coordinates.

We read clocks.

We distinguish yesterday from tomorrow.

We inherit numbers whose inventors are nameless to us.

We use grammatical forms older than the countries whose languages now carry them.

The machinery disappears into availability.

That is why knowledge can become ordinary.

And now the 54 return in a different light.

They are not fifty-four subjects.

They are not fifty-four kinds of reality.

They are not fifty-four things that compete with geometry, language, history or mathematics.

They are the forms by which something in any of those worlds becomes enough to be grasped.

Enough to stand.

Enough to bear.

Enough to do.

Enough to survive translation.

Enough to survive its speaker.

Enough to become ground.

That is why the fifty-four are the domain-generating domain.

Geometry can grow because completed geometrical intelligibilities can become ground for further geometry.

Language can grow because completed intelligibilities can pass between minds.

Knowledge can grow because a completion can cross generations.

Culture can grow because what one human being reaches does not necessarily die with them.

And time itself becomes different for a creature capable of this.

We do not merely pass through it.

We inherit through it.

The past can remain available in the present.

And because it can, the present does not have to begin where the past began.

It can begin where the past managed to arrive.

A child can understand a theorem older than their language in its present form.

Think about that.

The sentence has crossed time.

The intelligibility has crossed languages.

Another mind receives it.

And the world becomes available from there.

This is shared reality at its longest reach.

Not two people agreeing in one room.

Human beings separated by continents, languages and centuries becoming able to stand in the same intelligible relation.

Pythagoras is gone.

The theorem is here.

The original utterances are gone.

The relation is here.

The original world is gone.

Enough remained.

And once enough remains, another human being can continue.

That may be the deepest ordinary miracle in the book.

The fairy tale does it.

The theorem does it.

A sentence does it.

A civilization does it.

Different meanings.

Different centuries.

Different languages.

Different worlds.

And still:

something becomes graspable,

takes a finite form,

remains available,

and another grasp can begin.

So the claim of the fifty-four has changed scale.

We began by asking how a sentence becomes enough.

We are now asking how a human world becomes able to accumulate.

Perhaps the answer has been in front of us from the beginning.

Whatever these fifty-four things are, they are enough for everything we know, because they are how we know.

And if that is right, the most astonishing thing about Pythagoras' theorem is not what the sentence means.

It is that after all this time,

in another human language,

it can still land.
