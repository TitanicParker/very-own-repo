Chapter Five

The Testing Field

Read this as a story first.

Do what you have always done.

  • He had watched the little red hen for many days, and he wished to catch her.
  • One morning, the little red hen went into her garden to gather sticks for the fire.
  • The sly fox crept from behind a bush.
  • He carried a large sack over his shoulder.
  • The little red hen heard a rustle in the leaves.
  • She turned quickly, but the fox sprang forward and caught her.
  • He popped her into the sack, tied it tightly, and set off through the wood toward his den.
  • The little red hen was frightened, but she was clever.

Eight consecutive sentences from The Sly Fox and the Little Red Hen.

You know what happened.

A fox has been watching a hen because he wants to catch her. She goes into the garden. He approaches quietly. She hears him too late. He catches her, secures her in a sack, and begins carrying her away.

Then the story gives you one more thing.

She is frightened. But she is clever.

That last sentence is already doing something to you.

The sack is tied. The fox is on his way home. The hen has been caught.

And yet she was clever makes another future available.

The story can continue because what has just become intelligible changes what can become intelligible next.

This is so ordinary that it is difficult to see.

We say that stories move forward.

Characters act. Things happen. One scene follows another.

But underneath that movement, completion is continually making continuation possible.

Go back to the first sentence.

He had watched the little red hen for many days, and he wished to catch her.

The watching does not remain mere watching. By the time the sentence completes, it has acquired an intention.

Now available
The fox wants the hen.

One morning, the little red hen went into her garden to gather sticks for the fire.

She is no longer simply the hen the fox wants. She is somewhere, doing something, for a reason.

The completed sentence leaves a present situation available.

Now available
The hen is in the garden, occupied with gathering sticks.

The sly fox crept from behind a bush.

The intention inherited from the first sentence can now enter the hen's present situation.

The fox is no longer merely watching from somewhere in the story. He has become active inside this scene.

Now available
The threat is present.

He carried a large sack over his shoulder.

A sack is a very small addition to the world.

But it enters a world in which the fox already wants to catch the hen and has already approached her.

Now available
The fox is equipped for what he intends.

The little red hen heard a rustle in the leaves.

Until this moment the danger belongs to us and to the fox.

Now something from that danger becomes available to the hen.

Not the whole truth. Just enough.

Now available
She has something to respond to.

She turned quickly, but the fox sprang forward and caught her.

Her response becomes part of the same completion in which it fails.

The fox's intention, his approach, the sack, and the rustle all acquire a new bearing because one thing has now happened.

Now available
The hen is caught.

He popped her into the sack, tied it tightly, and set off through the wood toward his den.

Three actions settle into one larger situation.

Inside. Secured. Moving.

Now available
The capture has become a journey.

The little red hen was frightened, but she was clever.

Fear belongs perfectly to the world we have inherited.

Cleverness does something more. It keeps another possibility alive inside the capture.

Now available
This may not be over.

Nothing in that second reading replaced the story.

The fox is still a fox. The hen is still in danger. You still want to know what happens next.

We have simply watched another phenomenon occurring at the same time.

Something becomes enough to be inherited.

That may be one of the deepest ordinary facts in this book.

The next thing does not become available from nowhere.

It becomes available in a world partly made by what has already become intelligible.

Completion changes the field of the possible next grasp.

This is why completed intelligibility matters beyond sentences.

Learning depends on things becoming clear enough to be carried.

Development depends on achieved capacities becoming conditions for new ones.

Progress depends on not having to begin again from nothing.

Understanding grows because something achieved can belong to what follows.

We need somewhere to test that claim.

Not examples manufactured for the fifty-four.

Not sentences written because we already know where we want them to land.

We need language with its own reasons for existing.

So we built a testing field.

Twenty-seven fairy tales.

Fairy tales are for being told.

They carry children and adults through houses, forests, bargains, dangers, repetitions, mistakes, rescues, transformations and returns.

They make us ask one of the oldest questions in language.

What happens next?

That innocence matters.

Cinderella was not written to demonstrate a Completion Topology.

Little Red Riding Hood was not designed to occupy a cell.

The Three Billy Goats Gruff do not care whether a coordinate can be recovered from them.

The stories are already busy being stories.

And because they are stories, inheritance is everywhere.

A warning matters because a danger has become available.

A disguise matters because an identity has already been established.

A return matters because somebody left.

A rescue matters because something became wrong enough to require one.

Narrative progress is completed intelligibility under the pressure of what happens next.

615 mathematical traversals.

The other half of the testing field could hardly feel more different.

It begins with extremely basic mathematical actions and constructions, then preserves the ordered realizations by which further relations become available.

Here the question is not what happens next. It is what can now be established.

Mathematics makes inheritance severe.

A story may surprise you and remain a story.

A mathematical step cannot quietly forget the conditions on which the next step depends.

What has been established has to remain available with enough fidelity for another lawful realization to inherit it.

Proof is completed intelligibility under the pressure of what follows.

So the two sides of the testing field are not here because they secretly mean the same thing.

They do not.

One gives us wolves, promises, porridge, shoes, forests and kings.

The other gives us constructions, relations, transformations and proof.

Their distance is part of the test.

A story progresses because what has happened can belong to what happens next.

A proof progresses because what has been established can belong to what is established next.

Learning, development and understanding show the same dependence in countless forms.

The next moment becomes available because something has become enough to be inherited.

Now the fifty-four change their role.

They are no longer waiting to be introduced.

They are tools with which we can inspect how a completion was achieved, what structure made it sufficient, and how the achieved result remains available.

The map is on the table. Now we walk the ground.

And we will not begin by forcing sentences into cells.

We begin with the sentence itself.

What was unresolved? What changed? What made the change complete? What remained available?

Only then do we ask where in the fifty-four we have landed.

The coordinate comes after the recovery.

The testing field can resist us.

A sentence may land cleanly. It may surprise us. It may make two cells look dangerously alike. It may expose a bad example, a weak definition, or a distinction we thought we understood.

Good.

That is what a testing field is for.

Twenty-seven fairy tales.

615 recoverable mathematical traversals.

The whole field of fifty-four available to us.

Now we find out whether the map survives the ground.
