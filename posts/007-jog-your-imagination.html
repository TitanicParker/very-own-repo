Swipe for more

Episode Seven

Jog Your Imagination

Episode Six ended with a promise: now we can return to the fifty-four and ask more precisely how a completion takes the shape it does.

We will.

But the last six episodes have made one other movement possible first. Things that were learned separately can now be put together.

Start with something from Episode Two.

An intelligibility can become perfectly clear and still be wrong. “The meeting is at three” can be understood, remembered, repeated and acted upon even if the meeting is actually at four.

Truth and intelligibility are not the same achievement.

Now add what Episode Six gave us.

Something that becomes complete can remain available to what follows. A settled grasp can become part of the opening condition for another one.

Put those two things together.

A mistaken completion can be inherited too.

That sounds obvious once it has been said. Its consequences are not small.

Something does not have to be true in order to become part of what another person, sentence, decision or action begins from.

Suppose somebody tells you:

“Your request is under review.”

That leaves one situation available.

Now suppose somebody instead tells you, “Your request has been approved.” That leaves a very different one.

Imagine that the evidence justified only the first sentence, but the second sentence was written.

The next person may not reopen the whole matter. They may begin from approved. Another message may inherit approved. A later decision may inherit it.

The mistake has acquired a future.

This gives us a darker version of the principle we have just learned.

Completion can make continuation possible. So can false completion.

The danger is not only that something wrong has been said. The danger is that something wrong can become stable enough for other things to continue from it.

That matters anywhere one completion is allowed to govern another.

It matters in an argument, a record, a promise, an explanation and a proof. It also matters when a machine is allowed to read what has been settled and use it as the opening condition for another action.

The old question remains: what became available here, and was it earned?

Mathematics makes that question unusually easy to see.

In the angle proof, the final equality was not enough merely because it was true. It had to become available through the route.

The third line could use what the first two had earned. The fourth could use what the third had earned.

The result and the route were not the same thing.

Now imagine that the proof is very old.

The person who first walked the route is gone. The room in which they worked is gone. Their voice, gestures, distractions and private experience are unavailable to you.

But the route can remain.

You can begin with the same opening conditions, accept one relation because it has been established, carry it into the next step, and eventually arrive where somebody else once arrived.

You have not entered that person's private consciousness.

Something more modest, and in its own way more astonishing, has happened.

A movement of understanding has survived the person in whom it first occurred.

The thinker did not survive inside the proof.

The route did.

That is a remarkable human achievement.

A proof can wait for another mind. So can a recipe, a map, a warning, a method, a story, a question or a sentence.

Something can become complete enough to leave the person in whom it first became clear and still take part in somebody else's future.

Writing cannot preserve the original experience intact.

But sometimes it preserves enough of an achieved intelligibility for another person to begin from it.

Someone can write a sentence and die. Centuries later, another person can read it and think something that would not otherwise have become possible.

The original completion has entered a future it could never have seen.

Teaching begins to look slightly different from here.

Perhaps a teacher does not primarily put knowledge into a learner. The teacher arranges conditions under which something can become complete for the learner too.

The learner does not receive understanding like a parcel. The learner gets somewhere.

A good explanation does not merely present the answer. It makes the route available enough that another person can arrive.

A good proof does this with unusual strictness. A good story can do it in a completely different way.

Different materials. Different pressures. The same strange possibility: what becomes clear in one life can sometimes participate in another.

This may be one way human beings manage to have anything like shared understanding at all.

We do not need identical minds or identical experiences.

We need enough of something to become available between us that we can both continue from it.

You know something I do not.

So you tell me.

If things go well, something that was available to you becomes available to me too. Nothing has been transferred whole, yet the difference between us has changed.

This happens so naturally that we usually call it conversation and move on.

“The meeting is at nine.” Now we can meet. “The path is flooded.” Now you may choose another route. “She already knows.” Now an entire conversation may proceed differently.

A few words can become load-bearing.

A sentence can therefore do more than describe a world.

Sometimes it changes the world that can responsibly be continued from.

That is why the distinction between an earned completion and an unearned one matters beyond the moment in which the sentence is understood.

Now return to the fifty-four.

The obvious objection has been waiting for us since Episode One.

Fifty-four sounds absurdly small beside everything human beings can understand.

How could fifty-four forms contain a proof, a betrayal, a joke, a childhood memory, a legal argument, a melody, a scientific discovery, a love affair or a civilization?

They could not.

And they do not need to.

The proposal has never required meaning itself to be small.

The world can remain inexhaustible. The things that can be thought, remembered, imagined, discovered, feared, desired and understood can remain inexhaustible too.

The finite proposal concerns the ways an intelligibility becomes graspable enough.

And a finite grammar would not imply a finite intellectual life.

One completion can become part of the opening condition for another. That one can become part of another. The compositions can deepen long after the local forms have begun to repeat.

A small grammar can participate in an indefinitely deep one.

A small alphabet does not produce a small literature.

A few musical intervals do not produce a few songs.

The comparison is not exact. It does not need to be. The point is that finitude in the local resources need not become finitude in what can be composed through them.

This is why Episode Six changes how the fifty-four can be imagined.

Before inheritance, a small grammar can look like a cabinet of forms.

After inheritance, something else becomes possible: completed forms can become conditions from which further completions begin.

The question then changes.

Not: how could fifty-four things explain everything?

How much complexity can arise when completed forms can be inherited, combined, nested, corrected, reopened and completed again?

Now widen the view again.

You did not invent the alphabet you are reading. You did not invent arithmetic. You did not discover from nothing that maps can preserve routes, that stories can outlive their tellers, or that a proof can make a relation available to somebody who was not there when it was first seen.

Almost everything you know arrived in a world already altered by previous human beings.

Somebody had already distinguished things, related things, corrected things, gathered things and settled things.

Enough of those achievements remained available for you not to have to begin again.

How much of civilization is possible because human beings learned to leave one another things that do not have to begin again?

That question is much larger than this episode can answer.

It is also difficult not to ask once completion and inheritance have been put beside each other.

Books begin to look different. Schools look different. Libraries, science, law and history begin to look different.

They all contain things that have become stable enough for later people to take them up.

Some remain good enough to inherit. Some have become stale. Some were mistaken from the beginning. Some survive only because nobody has yet reopened them.

Inheritance does not guarantee truth. It guarantees only that something has remained available.

Every generation receives a world partly structured by earlier landings.

Some are carried forward. Some are repaired. Some lose their governing force. Some become parts of larger wholes that could not have existed when the earlier completion occurred.

The future does not begin from nothing.

None of this proves the fifty-four.

It does something else.

It lets us see what would be at stake if a small recurrent grammar of completion really were there.

Perhaps the astonishing possibility is not that fifty-four forms contain the world.

Perhaps it is that an inexhaustible world can keep becoming graspable through a small number of recurring ways of becoming enough.

That would not make meaning small. It would make intelligibility remarkably economical.

There is a future in which none of this feels particularly exotic.

Not because everybody has been persuaded by a theory, but because the phenomenon has become easier to notice.

The question might gradually stop being “Do I believe in this grammar?” and become something more ordinary.

What just became complete?

What did that completion leave available?

What can now continue from it?

What would have to happen for it to be reopened?

Perhaps that is the larger ambition.

Not to make you memorize a hidden structure.

To make an event you already undergo constantly visible enough that, once noticed, it becomes difficult to stop noticing.

There is one final thing to notice before we return to the fifty-four.

You have been reading this book in gulps. Episode Two already told you that the swipe does not mean the subject is finished; it means this much can now be carried into the next much.

You have been physically practising inheritance while the book taught you to see it.

And the episodes have been doing the same thing at a larger scale.

Meaning became distinguishable from intelligibility. Enough became portable. The primitive grammar became visible. The whole field appeared. Story exposed inheritance. Proof made that inheritance load-bearing.

This episode could begin only because those earlier completions remained available together.

So when we return to the fifty-four, there is another question underneath the one we were going to ask.

Not only:

What shape did this completion take?

What future did that shape make available?

That question was not available at the beginning of this book in the way it is available now.

Something had to be completed first.

Jog your imagination.

Where does yours go now?
