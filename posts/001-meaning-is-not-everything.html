Essay 001

Meaning Is Not Everything

You are already doing the thing this essay is about. That is why it is so difficult to see.

A starting point for the blog.

Something has already happened between us. You read that sentence and understood it. You did not have to stop halfway through and ask yourself what kind of mental operation would allow the words to become intelligible. You did not inspect the sentence, identify the particular shape of understanding it required, perform that shape deliberately, and then collect the meaning at the end. You simply read it. The meaning was there for you, almost immediately, and whatever had to happen for it to be there happened so naturally that it did not present itself as a separate event at all. The same thing is happening now. And now. You are not merely looking at words. You are continuously realizing intelligibilities, one giving way to another, carrying enough of what has already become clear for the next thing to become clear in turn. We have ordinary names for how successful this feels. Fluency is one. Clarity is another. Neither tells us very much about how extraordinary it is.

The strange thing about language is not that it sometimes works. The strange thing is how relentlessly it works. We speak while walking, driving, washing dishes, looking for keys, watching somebody’s face, thinking about what we are going to say next. We read messages while standing in queues. We listen to people who hesitate, interrupt themselves, change direction, choose the wrong word and correct it. We understand sentences we have never encountered before and ideas that have never previously existed in exactly that form. Most of the time we do not congratulate ourselves on any of this. We do not even notice it. Meaning simply keeps arriving. The success is so complete that the mechanism of success has become transparent.

This blog begins inside that transparency.

You are reading for meaning. That is what readers do. If I tell you that somebody left a bag on a train and came back three hours later to find it still on the seat, you are already somewhere inside the story. You may wonder what was in the bag, why nobody took it, whether the person was lucky, whether you would have gone back for it. Your own memories and expectations begin working immediately. The sentence opens onto a world, and the world is more interesting than the sentence. This is exactly as it should be. But there is something else occurring so successfully that your attention passes straight through it. Before any of those personal realizations can matter to you, what I have written must become intelligible enough for you to have them.

The finite thing is not meaning. The finite thing may be the way meaning becomes graspable.

That distinction matters because a finite structure of intelligibility does not imply a finite world of meaning. It implies almost the opposite. Think of light passing through a prism. The prism does not invent everything illuminated by the light, and it does not decide what anybody will see in the colours. It provides a structure through which variation can appear. Human beings bring astonishing differences to understanding. Two people can hear the same sentence, understand it perfectly, and realize very different things through it. One remembers a childhood room. Another sees a practical consequence. Another hears an insult. Another recognizes a joke. The shared intelligibility has not erased their individuality. It has given their individuality something clear enough to work with.

For most of our lives there is no reason to separate meaning from the grasping structure that makes meaning available. If I tell you to put the blue cup beside the kettle, you do not need a theory of intelligibility. You need to know which cup I mean and where to put it. If somebody says, “I didn’t mean Tuesday, I meant Thursday,” you do not need to know what structural form has just been repaired. You correct your understanding and continue. Human beings were exquisitely fluent long before anybody needed the vocabulary I am going to use here. We still do not need it. You could read every essay in this repository, forget every technical term, and understand people just as well tomorrow morning.

That is part of what makes this worth looking at.

There are many things we study because they are difficult. This is difficult to see for almost the opposite reason. We are too good at it. The machinery is hidden by success. Meaning does not merely occupy our attention; it rewards it. Understanding something is satisfying. A question becomes clear, a distinction suddenly matters, two things fall into relation, a mistaken arrangement is corrected, scattered parts become one thing, a lingering uncertainty finally settles. We experience the satisfaction of the intelligibility itself. We do not ordinarily turn around inside that satisfaction and ask what shape it had.

But we can.

Take the smallest possible example. Someone points into a crowded photograph and says, “Her.” Nothing grand has happened. One face among many has become the face that matters. Or imagine someone saying, “This one, not that one.” The selected thing now makes sense partly through the alternative it excludes. Or consider the words, “Only if it rains.” Whatever follows is not simply floating free; its intelligibility now carries a condition. Or, “That explains everything.” Earlier things that were already present acquire a new bearing together. These are ordinary moments. We pass through them constantly. Yet they do not satisfy us in the same way. The grasp has different shapes.

Enough is an important word here. Intelligibility does not require the end of the world. A sentence can be complete and still belong to a paragraph. A paragraph can be complete and still belong to an argument. A decision can settle one question and create another. A repaired bicycle can break again next week. A destination can be reached and immediately become the starting point of another journey. Completion, in the sense that matters here, is not absolute finality. It is the satisfaction of the particular demand that makes the present intelligibility what it is. Something has become clear enough to be taken, carried, related, acted upon, remembered, questioned, contradicted or continued.

We are constantly being satisfied in this way. We read novels, instructions, menus, legal agreements, love letters, jokes, equations, headlines and text messages. We listen to arguments and apologies. We tell stories. We give directions. We mutter to ourselves. We explain things to children. We lie. We misunderstand and recover. We make promises. We ask whether there is milk in the fridge. Across all of this extraordinary variety, intelligibility keeps finding ways to become enough.

The proposal behind this work is that those ways are not limitless. Meaning may be. The things that can be thought, noticed, imagined, remembered, invented, desired, feared, discovered and communicated appear inexhaustible. But the forms by which an intelligibility becomes graspable are astonishingly small in number. Under the immense variety of meaning there appears to be a tiny recurrent grammar. It does not tell us what to think. It does not tell us what anything means. It does not reduce one person’s realization to another’s. It tells us something much stranger: the ways in which meaning becomes enough for us have a structure.

One way of stating the structure is almost suspiciously simple. An intelligibility can perform one of six basic kinds of work. The way that work becomes enough has three basic orientations. And what is realized through it can remain available in three basic ways. Six by three by three. Fifty-four.

The immediate response should probably be disbelief of a particular sort: not “that sounds impossibly complicated,” but “surely somebody would have noticed.” Exactly. If something this ordinary has such a small structure, how could it remain unseen?

The answer may be embarrassingly simple. Nobody needed to see it.

A child does not need a theory of balance to learn not to fall over. You do not need to know the anatomy of your hand in order to pick up a glass. And nobody needs a table of completed intelligibilities to understand “Pass me the salt.” The competence is prior to its description. The thing works whether or not anybody has named its workings. In fact, the better it works, the less reason there is to interrupt it with inspection.

Fluency is what invisibility feels like when the structure is working. Clarity is what successful intelligibility feels like from inside the grasp.

There is one more reason this is a good place to begin. You will not learn this by asking a GPT to tell you what it already knows. A language model is extremely good at meaning, and that turns out to be part of the problem. Give one a sentence and ask what it means, and meaning rushes forward. Ask what the sentence is doing structurally and meaning often rushes forward again. The same compelling success that hides these forms from ordinary human attention can hide them inside systems built to handle language.

This work emerged through months of trying to make that distinction clear enough to teach. The task eventually had to be broken apart. One model was asked to attend to a sentence without knowing the grammar and describe what the sentence itself was doing. Another was asked to recover a structural coordinate from that frozen description. A third was given only the coordinate and asked to derive the form in the opposite direction. The separation was not ceremony. It was a response to the fact that the thing we were trying to see kept disappearing into meaning.

That return trip will matter later. For now, there is only one thing I want you to notice.

You are still reading. Meaning is still arriving. And underneath it, almost perfectly invisible, something keeps becoming enough.
